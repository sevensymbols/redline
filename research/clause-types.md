# Redline research: which clause types actually burn people

Searches used: 9 of 12. Pages read (WebFetch): 9 of 15 (2 returned unusable content: FU 2017 PDF was garbled binary; WCC PDFs returned only an index page).
Verification key: [READ] = figure seen in a page I fetched. [SNIPPET] = figure appeared only in a search-result summary; I did not confirm it on the page, so treat as unverified.

## Findings

1. Auto-renewal / negative-option subscriptions: complaint volume is rising. [READ]
   - Claim: "nearly 70 consumer complaints per day on average" in 2024, versus "42 per day" in 2021; "steadily increasing over the past five years". The rule targets failure to disclose material terms, no express consent, and no simple cancel mechanism.
   - Limit: this is a count of complaints about negative-option practices overall (not only contract clauses), and the FTC release gives no comparison to other clause types.
   - Source: https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2024/10/federal-trade-commission-announces-final-click-cancel-rule-making-it-easier-consumers-end-recurring

2. Freelance nonpayment and late payment are very common (NY survey). [READ]
   - Claim: 62% of NY freelance workers lost wages at least once to nonpayment; "91% of survey respondents reported experiencing late or overdue wages at least once"; 54% had delays of 3 months or longer; 53% lost as much as $10,000; 39% said it affected rent or bills; less than 1% used the legal system. The proposed fix includes written contracts for work of $250 or more and payment within 30 days "unless otherwise agreed".
   - Limit: NY-only, self-selected survey. It measures nonpayment, not which clause caused it.
   - Source: https://blog.freelancersunion.org/2022/05/12/over-60-of-ny-freelancers-report-not-being-paid-for-work-performed/

3. Late or missing payment affects new freelancers early. [READ, secondhand]
   - Claim: "47% of freelancers reported at least one late or missing client payment in their first six months", cited by Freelancers Union to Genius's 2025 freelance stats (I did not see the Genius original).
   - Source: https://blog.freelancersunion.org/2026/01/27/why-most-freelancers-fail-at-money-management-within-six-months-according-to-data/

4. Older national freelancer payment data. [SNIPPET, unverified]
   - Claim as shown in search summary: 71% of freelancers cite trouble collecting payment at some point; in 2014, about half had trouble, average loss about $6,000. The Freelancers Union 2017 nonpayment report PDF could not be parsed.
   - Source: https://www.onlabor.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/05/FU_NonpaymentReport_r3.pdf

5. Arbitration and class-action waivers are widespread in financial contracts, and consumers do not understand them. [READ for the first three figures; the 75% and under-7% figures are SNIPPET only]
   - Claim: 62% of the top 50 banks have arbitration clauses in checking contracts (7.7% of all banks); about 9 in 10 arbitration clauses bar class actions; fewer than 1,250 consumer arbitrations were filed in 2010-2012 across the markets studied. Snippet only: 75% of credit-card consumers surveyed did not know whether they were subject to an arbitration clause.
   - Limit: this is prevalence of the clause, not complaint frequency. The study covers financial products only.
   - Source: https://www.consumerfinance.gov/archive/newsroom/the-cfpb-finds-few-consumers-file-arbitration-cases/

6. Non-competes cover a large share of workers. [READ]
   - Claim: "An estimated* 18% of U.S. workers are covered by noncompetes"; the FTC estimated a ban "could increase wages by nearly $300 billion per year" and expand opportunities for about 30 million Americans. The rule was later vacated (per the FTC statement titled "...acceding vacatur" that appeared in search results; I did not read it).
   - Limit: employee-focused. Prevalence only, not complaint counts. Relevance to Redline's freelancer and consumer users is partial.
   - Source: https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2023/01/ftc-proposes-rule-ban-noncompete-clauses-which-hurt-workers-harm-competition

7. Security deposit terms are the top tenant complaint at one student legal-aid office, but with no counts. [READ; anecdotal]
   - Claim: attorney quote that "'my landlord is not giving me the security deposit back' is the most common complaint the staff hears from students every year"; he "could not say how many students complained ... because it did not have a complete log". Named lease problems: deposits relabelled as non-refundable "administrative fees", vague damage charges ("cleaning, painting, et cetera"), and notice given by portal instead of the legally required method.
   - Source: https://cu-citizenaccess.org/2026/09/security-deposit-disputes-most-common-complaint-received-by-student-legal-services/

8. In B2B contracts, liability caps, indemnity and price changes are the most negotiated terms. [SNIPPET, unverified, and measures negotiation friction, not harm]
   - Claim as shown in search summary: World Commerce & Contracting ranks limitation of liability #1, indemnification #2, price/price changes #3, termination #4, scope #5, warranty #6, performance #7, payment #8, data protection #9, liquidated damages #10. I could not open the report to confirm the ranking or the survey basis.
   - Source: https://www.worldcc.com/Portals/IACCM/Reports/Most-Negotiated-Terms-2024.pdf

## Ranked table (what the evidence supports)

No source gave a head-to-head count of complaints by clause type, so this is a rough ordering, not a measured frequency ranking. Each row's basis is labelled.

| Rank | Clause type | Basis for rank | Evidence type |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Payment terms / nonpayment / late payment (freelance) | Highest quantified incidence of any harm I found: 62% lost wages, 91% late or overdue (NY survey, F2); 47% late or missing in first six months (F3) | Quantitative, but self-selected freelancer surveys; measures the outcome, not a specific clause |
| 2 | Auto-renewal / cancellation (negative option) | About 70 FTC complaints per day, rising from 42 per day (F1) | Quantitative (regulator data), but covers all negative-option practices, so it is a ceiling for the clause |
| 3 | Arbitration / class-action waiver | About 90% of arbitration clauses bar class actions; most consumers unaware (F5) | Quantitative prevalence, not complaint counts; finance only |
| 4 | Non-compete | 18% of workers covered (F6) | Quantitative prevalence, employee context |
| 5 | Security deposit / lease fees | Most common complaint at one legal-aid office (F7) | Anecdotal, no counts |
| 6 | Limitation of liability, indemnity, price changes (B2B) | Most negotiated terms (F8) | Unverified snippet; negotiation frequency, not harm |
| Unranked | IP assignment, kill fees, scope creep / unlimited revisions, fee escalators, unilateral termination, early termination fees | No source I read gave reliable counts | None usable (see below) |

## What I could not find

- No dataset ranks clause types by complaint frequency. FTC Consumer Sentinel, CFPB and BBB data are categorised by industry or practice, not by contract clause. The BBB search returned only historical industry counts (cell phones, car dealers), which I did not read.
- No quantified source for: IP assignment, kill fees, scope creep / unlimited revisions, fee escalators, unilateral termination, early termination fees, indemnity (as a complaint), or net-60+ terms specifically. Search results showed secondary-blog statistics (e.g. "68% of freelancers have unknowingly signed away IP rights", "28% use a written contract", "52% of projects suffer scope creep"). I did not verify or use them: they are uncited or traceable only to marketing blogs.
- The CFPB 2025 figure of 2,393 complaints mentioning arbitration appeared only in a search snippet; I did not open the report, so I excluded it.
- The Freelancers Union 2017 nonpayment report and the WCC reports could not be read (garbled PDF / index page only).
- Legal-aid reports with counts (tenant or small-claims) were not found; security deposit evidence is anecdotal.
- Forum threads (Reddit etc.) were not searched, so there is no community-level anecdote data beyond F7.
- Caveat for the demand hypothesis: the strongest evidence is about nonpayment behaviour and subscription practices, which a document-reading tool can only partly address (it can flag payment terms and renewal clauses, but not prevent a client from simply not paying).
